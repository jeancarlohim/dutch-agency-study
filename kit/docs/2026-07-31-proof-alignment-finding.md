# The finding: alignment, not narrowness

This is the answer the whole study was circling. It only appeared once we
measured what an agency PROVES separately from what it SAYS.

2,696 client cases were catalogued across 327 agencies — every case tagged with
client, industry, business model, named-or-anonymous, and whether it carries a
number. Then each agency's evidence was judged for direction: does the case list
point at one market, or scatter?

## The four-way split (n=218 with growth data)

| | n | % growing | median growth | p90 | median proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SAY narrow + **PROVE narrow** (aligned) | 49 | **59%** | **+20%** | **+132%** | **1.0** |
| SAY broad + PROVE scattered (honest generalist) | 91 | 56% | +11% | +66% | 0.0 |
| SAY broad + PROVE narrow (silent niche) | 41 | 54% | +4% | +50% | 0.0 |
| SAY narrow + **PROVE scattered** (mismatch) | 37 | **35%** | **0%** | +100% | 0.0 |

**The gap between the top and bottom rows is 24 points — and both of them claim
a niche.** The only difference is whether the case studies back the claim.

## What this means

**Claiming a niche you cannot prove is the worst position in the Dutch agency
market.** Worse than being honestly, openly broad (35% vs 56% growing; median
0% vs +11%). A narrow message with scattered evidence underperforms saying
nothing specific at all.

That reframes the entire advice. It is not "narrow your message." It is:

> Narrow the message and the proof together, or don't narrow the message.
> A claim your case studies contradict costs you more than no claim.

And it explains why every earlier test came back null. Message narrowness alone
correlates -0.032 with growth. Proof concentration alone correlates +0.021.
Neither main effect exists — but their INTERACTION separates 35% from 59%. We
kept testing the ingredients separately and finding nothing, because the signal
only lives in whether they match.

## On the silent-niche hypothesis specifically

JC's idea — broad message casting a wide net while the proof quietly concentrates
— is real and common (41 agencies, 19% of the market) but it is NOT the winning
strategy. It sits mid-pack: 54% growing, median +4%, and the lowest bookability
in the study at 10%. Casting wide while proving narrow does not out-earn simply
being aligned. Worth reporting honestly rather than promoting.

## What the market actually proves

Of 270 agencies with catalogued evidence: **156 scattered, 70 leaning, 44 laser.**
So 58% of Dutch agencies have client evidence that points nowhere.

Where the concentrated ones point:

| direction | agencies |
|---|---|
| e-commerce | 27 |
| public sector | 24 |
| hospitality | 5 |
| local trades | 5 |
| solo coaches | 5 |

## Caveats to state plainly

- n=37 in the mismatch cell and n=49 in the aligned cell. Directional, not
  definitive.
- Correlation, not causation. An agency that is already winning in one market
  naturally accumulates concentrated proof — the arrow may point backwards.
- `laser` concentration alone underperforms `leaning` (44% vs 65% growing),
  which is odd and probably reflects small case-counts inflating "laser".
  Do not build a claim on the laser/leaning distinction.
- Everything rests on Sales Nav headcount as the growth measure, which remains
  a poor proxy for revenue or margin.

## Why this matters for the Sprint

The Sprint does not hand a client a niche and tell them to announce it. It
builds the offer FROM their existing call recordings and past clients — that is,
it derives the message from the evidence that already exists.

This finding says that is the correct sequence. The agencies in the worst
position in this market are precisely the ones who did it the other way round:
picked a positioning, published the claim, and never built the case studies to
match. 37 of them, growing at half the rate of everyone else.

**The line for the article:** *"The riskiest thing you can do is claim a niche
your case studies don't back up. In this study those agencies grew at 35% —
worse than the ones who never claimed anything."*
